> In contrast, protein was not correlated with any of the measured environmental variables, including total soil nitrogen and organic carbon at the source sites, and only weakly and negatively with grain weight (Fig. 1c, d). This pattern suggests that grain protein is governed by processes other than those organizing carbohydrate composition. A negative relationship between grain yield or grain weight and grain protein concentration is well documented (Simmonds 1995) and can be explained by a dilution effect: carbohydrates accumulate faster than nitrogen during grain filling, so protein becomes a smaller proportion of total grain mass (Bogard et al. 2010). Differences between wild and cultivated barley in dry-matter accumulation, nitrogen allocation and nitrogen harvest index further showed that grain protein depends on the balance between carbon deposition and nitrogen acquisition rather than on direct competition with starch alone (Corke et al. 1989). This separation has a practical implication: the strong starch–fiber trade-off may not constrain protein, so increasing fiber or β-glucan need not reduce protein. At the genomic level, none of the protein candidate genes could be tested, so the genetic control of protein remains unresolved in this panel<sup>(TODO 402)</sup>.
