## Change 1 of 5 · Discussion §2 ¶2 ("The trade-off also had…"), last three sentences (6.1 + names)

*Why:* 6.1: *"where a region could not be resolved, nothing contradicted it"* uses the absence of evidence as support, so it goes. The 1H sentence stays as a neutral fact, because the paragraph opens with "three regions… resolved at three different levels" and 1H is the third (see Q1). The two 3H genes also get their short names, so the reader connects them with Results Ch. 3 and Table 2.

**With the changes marked:**

The trade-off also had a genomic counterpart. Fiber and starch loci co-localized in three regions, and these resolved at three different levels. On chromosome 7H, the two loci resolved to a single gene, and a single haplotype of this gene carried both effects, higher fiber and lower starch of similar magnitude. With the two lead SNPs only 154 bp apart, this pattern is more simply explained by one causal variant expressed through two correlated traits than by two linked genes. Moreover, the carriers of the minor alleles at this locus were drawn from across the ecological range of the collection rather than concentrated in the desert populations <sup>(TODO 403)</sup>, so this shared signal cannot be attributed to the geographic origin of the accessions. The gene encodes a GDSL esterase/lipase rather than a starch enzyme, so its link to starch is more likely compositional than mechanistic, as a shift in the cell-wall fraction changes the share of starch in the grain in the opposite direction. On chromosome 3H, the two neighbouring loci resolved to two genes, one per trait, a cutin-biosynthetic acyltransferase<ins style="color:#1e8449;background:#e9f7ef;text-decoration:none"> (*GPAT6*)</ins> for fiber and a plastid phosphate transporter<ins style="color:#1e8449;background:#e9f7ef;text-decoration:none"> (*PHT4;3*)</ins> for starch. On chromosome 1H, the region contained no gene with a known role in either trait. **With the changes marked:**

Statistical rank was a poor guide to biological candidacy. The most significant genes were often the least interpretable, unannotated or derived from mobile elements, whereas the genes with a clear route to the trait ranked lower. Because genes within a locus share one linkage-disequilibrium block, their significance largely reflects linkage with the lead signal rather than their own function, so significance identifies the locus, and biological relevance has to choose the gene. Two steps of the analysis can also miss genuine candidates. The haplotype analysis requires sufficient variation within a gene to define haplotype groups, so a gene that could not be tested is not a gene without effect.
